The case for slower software

For years, software has borrowed its moral language from speed. Faster onboarding. Faster notifications. Faster generation. Faster answers. The promise is seductive: if the tool moves quickly enough, perhaps we can finally catch up with ourselves.

But much of the work that matters is not improved by acceleration alone. Deciding what to write, who to call, what to ignore, and which obligation deserves our best energy are not throughput problems. They are attention problems. When software treats every pause as friction, it quietly edits reflection out of the process.

Good tools should reduce unnecessary effort without removing the useful resistance that helps us think.

Slower software is not clumsy software. It is software with humane defaults: fewer alerts, clearer boundaries, visible state, recoverable mistakes, and enough quiet for the user to form an intention before acting. The future I want is not less capable. It is less frantic.
